## WHAT APPEARS POSSIBLE

**Adding one or more ADUs is the realistic pathway. It is also the only one
that isn't penalised by your property's housing history.**

Under SB 1211, a lot with existing multifamily dwellings may add detached ADUs
up to the number of existing units — four, in your case — plus conversion of
non-livable existing space at 25% of the unit count, which is one more. That is
the *statutory ceiling*.

**It is not what your lot can hold.** You have 5,974.5 sq ft carrying a 4,092
sq ft building. Four detached ADUs is not a credible program on that footprint.
A realistic outcome is one detached ADU, or one conversion, or possibly both,
and the honest answer depends on a measured survey we do not have.

Two costs to price in before you design anything: the parcel is in a **Methane
Zone** (mitigation required for new construction) and a **Special Grading
Area** under BOE Basic Grid Map A-13372 (grading review). Neither is unusual on
the Westside; both are real line items.

Why this route and not the others: every housing flag on your parcel penalises
*removing or altering* the existing four units, and **none of them penalises
adding new ones**. That asymmetry is the single most useful thing the record
tells us.

**A second, narrower possibility worth knowing exists.** Your parcel is flagged
**ED 1 Eligible**, sits in the **Highest** TCAC Opportunity Area, and is a
**Very Low Vehicle Travel Area** under AB 2334. Stacked together, those three
designations mean a **100% affordable** project here would not face the density
ceiling that otherwise constrains this lot. That is a different business from
adding units to your own building — it implies an affordable developer and a
financing stack — and we raise it only because the designations genuinely line
up, and nothing else on this parcel does.

## WHAT DOES NOT APPEAR TO APPLY

**SB 1123 — confirmed out.** It applies to single-family-zoned lots. Your
zoning is `[Q]R3-1-O`, a multifamily zone. Settled; you can stop tracking it.
(Worth noting the usual reason given — "your lot isn't vacant" — is out of
date: state guidance from October 2025 allows a lot with a building to qualify
via a remainder parcel. The zoning is what closes it, not the building.)

**SB 9 — confirmed out**, for the same reason: single-family zones only.

**CHIP's Mixed Income Incentive Program — confirmed out.** The City's record
returns **Not Eligible** on all three MIIP geographies: Transit Oriented
Incentive Area, Opportunity Corridors, and Corridor Transition. `Transit
Oriented Communities` also returns Not Eligible.

**SB 79 and the City's Low-Rise Ordinance — do not reach your parcel.** Both
key to proximity to a qualifying major transit stop, and the City's record
states you are **not** within half a mile of one.

One field on your report looks like it says otherwise: `High Quality Transit
Corridor (within ½ mile): Yes`. That is a *corridor* test — frequent bus
service on a nearby street — and it is a different thing from being near a
qualifying *stop*. It does not confer transit incentives. Four other rows on
the same report confirm the negative.

**SB 684 — appears closed, and not for the reason we first expected.** SB 684
gives ministerial approval to small subdivisions on multifamily lots, and your
zoning would ordinarily qualify. Three verified facts appear to close it:
`Housing Use within Prior 5 Years: Yes`; an **Ellis Act filing dated 15 May
2017**; and affirmative replacement flags (`HCA / Resident Protections
Ordinance Replacement Review: Yes`, `HE Replacement Required: Yes`). Together
these make the existing units protected housing, which SB 684 bars a project
from demolishing or altering.

There is also a plainer obstacle: on 5,974.5 sq ft already carrying a 4,092 sq
ft building, there is very little left to subdivide even if the law allowed it.

## WHAT NEEDS VERIFICATION

**1. The `[Q]` condition on your zoning — the most important open item.**
Your zone is `[Q]R3-1-O`. The bracketed `[Q]` is a qualified condition whose
text is not in the parcel report. Your record also lists case
**CPC-1988-341-ZC**, described as a zone change *"to limit the land so
designated to the RD1.5 density"* in the vicinity of Pico and Beverwil.

If that condition governs your parcel, permitted density is roughly **three
units** on your lot area — against four existing, which would make your
building legal nonconforming with **no residual density**. Unqualified R3 would
instead allow about seven.

We are not going to tell you which it is, because the report does not link the
case to the condition and we have not read the ordinance. **Until that document
is pulled, no one should quote you a unit count** — including us.

**2. Why your property shows `RSO: No`.** A 1947 four-unit building in the City
of Los Angeles would ordinarily fall under the Rent Stabilization Ordinance.
Your record says it does not. The record states the fact and **not the reason**,
and we are not going to guess at it. The 2017 Ellis filing is the most obvious
candidate, but the report asserts no connection between the two fields. This
needs LAHD.

**This matters more than it sounds.** `RSO: No` does **not** mean the property
is unencumbered:

- `Just Cause for Eviction Ordinance: Yes` — protections apply after the
  initial lease expires or six months of continuous occupancy.
- `HCA / Resident Protections Ordinance Replacement Review: Yes`.
- `HE Replacement Required: Yes` — your parcel is a Housing Element site.
- The Ellis filing remains on the record.

**3. The Ellis Act history and its current effect.** Filed 15 May 2017. Ellis
carries re-rental restrictions, former-tenant rights, and a look-back period
during which withdrawn units count as protected units. We have a secondary
description of that look-back, **not the ordinance text**, so we are
deliberately not putting a date in front of you. Confirm with LAHD — and ask
specifically whether any deadline is approaching, because if the look-back
works the way the secondary sources describe, timing could matter.

**4. LADBS permit history and certificate of occupancy.** Confirms all four
units are legally permitted — which matters, because the ADU count is computed
from *legal* units — and surfaces any unpermitted work before you open a file
with the City.

**5. A measured existing-conditions survey.** Footprint, coverage, setbacks,
parking, open space. This is what converts "up to four detached ADUs" into a
real number.

## MOST IMPORTANT NEXT STEPS

**First, pull the `[Q]` condition.** One ordinance decides whether you have
development capacity at all. Everything else is secondary to it. City Planning
can provide the condition text against case CPC-1988-341-ZC and the related
ordinances on your record.

**Second, call LAHD** with two questions: why does APN 4330-005-041 show
`RSO: No`, and what is the current effect of the 15 May 2017 Ellis filing —
including any date by which its consequences change.

**Third, if you are going to spend design money, spend it on the ADU study.**
It is the only pathway the record does not penalise. Start with the survey, not
a scheme.

Two things worth saying plainly.

The programs you asked about — SB 684, SB 1123, CHIP — were written for vacant
land, single-family parcels, or transit-adjacent sites. Your property is none
of those. That is a real reason they were hard to apply, and it is not a gap in
your reading of them.

And the constraint here is not zoning. It is your property's **housing
history** — the Ellis filing, the Housing Element designation, the replacement
flags. That is an unusual place to look for a development constraint, and it is
where a zoning-first analysis goes wrong.
