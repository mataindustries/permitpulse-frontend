## WHAT APPEARS POSSIBLE

**1. Adding ADUs under the multifamily ADU rules — the most promising path.**

SB 1211, in effect since 1 January 2025, changed what an existing multifamily
property can do. On a lot with existing multifamily dwellings you may add
**detached ADUs up to the number of existing units** (four units → up to four
detached), **plus** conversion of non-livable existing space — storage,
boiler rooms, garages, carports — into ADUs at **25% of the existing unit
count, minimum one**. The two categories are counted separately and combine.
Local agencies may not require replacement of uncovered off-street parking
removed for ADU construction.

Why this matters more than the programs you asked about: it does **not**
require demolishing or altering the existing units. That single characteristic
keeps it clear of the tenant-protection and replacement-housing rules that
appear to obstruct the other pathways. It is also ministerial — a permit, not
an entitlement.

The statutory ceiling is not a site capacity. What the lot can actually absorb
depends on lot area, existing footprint, open space and setbacks, none of
which we have yet.

**2. Density bonus under CHIP — with one number to get first.**

The Citywide Housing Incentive Program (adopted 7 February 2025, operative
11 February 2025) consolidated LA's density-bonus framework into three
subprograms: State Density Bonus, the Mixed Income Incentive Program (MIIP),
and the Affordable Housing Incentive Program (AHIP, for 100% affordable
projects, citywide). MIIP is geographically gated — Transit Oriented Incentive
Areas, Opportunity Corridors, and Opportunity Corridor Transition Areas — and
we have not established whether your parcel sits in any of them.

Before any of that matters: **every CHIP project requires a Replacement Unit
Determination from LAHD.** If the existing units are rent-stabilized, removed
RSO units must be replaced one-for-one as covenanted affordable units
regardless of the prior tenants' incomes. On a lot of this size that
obligation could consume the entire density you would gain. Get that
determination before anything else on this track.

**3. The City's new Low-Rise Ordinance — check this before anything else.**

You did not raise this one, and it is the newest thing on the board.

The background matters. SB 79 took effect statewide on **1 July 2026**,
allowing substantially higher residential density within a half mile of
qualifying transit stops. But SB 79 lets a city delay application on certain
properties or adopt an alternative transit-oriented plan preserving citywide
capacity — and **Los Angeles used that provision**, deferring citywide SB 79
application to roughly **2030**. In its place the City adopted a **Low-Rise
Ordinance** and a **Phased Implementation Ordinance**, both effective
**30 June 2026**.

So the practical question is not whether you are in an SB 79 tier. It is
**whether you are in a Low-Rise Ordinance station area.** That ordinance
permits two-to-four-story multifamily at more than fifty station areas, with
an eight-foot rear setback, and exempts HPOZs and Historic-Cultural Monuments.

Whether your parcel qualifies is a map question, not a reasoning question. The
nearest Metro rail looks to be over half a mile away. But qualification can
also run through the intersection of two major bus routes at twenty-minute
peak headways or better, and if a buffer clips any part of a parcel the whole
parcel counts — which makes Pico and Robertson worth checking rather than
assuming.

The City publishes both the Low-Rise and SB 79 eligibility maps in ZIMAS.
Check them in the same session as everything else. Two caveats worth knowing:
the City's deferral of SB 79 is being challenged, so the 2030 horizon is not
settled; and if the property were ever identified as a historic resource, that
is one of the categories a city may exclude.

## WHAT DOES NOT APPEAR TO APPLY

**SB 1123 — probably not, but not for the reason you would expect.**
This is the 2024 amendment extending the Starter Home Revitalization Act to
**vacant** lots in **single-family** zones under 1.5 acres.

The obvious answer is that your lot is not vacant — it has a building on it.
**That answer is out of date.** HCD guidance issued **7 October 2025** holds
that a single-family lot with an existing home can still qualify as "vacant"
where the subdivision designates a remainder parcel for the portion holding
the existing structure; the balance of the site is then treated as vacant.

So SB 1123 turns entirely on the **zoning** question, which we have not
verified. If your parcel is multifamily-zoned, SB 1123 is the wrong tool and
SB 684 is the relevant one. If it is single-family-zoned, SB 1123 is live and
needs a fresh look. Confirm the zone before setting this down.

**SB 9.** Urban lot splits and two-unit developments apply only in
single-family zones. A multifamily-zoned parcel is outside its scope.
*(Same zoning caveat.)*

**SB 684, as it is usually understood — with one real caveat below.**
SB 684 gives ministerial approval, on a 60-day clock, to subdivisions of ten
or fewer parcels and ten or fewer units on multifamily lots under five acres,
with new parcels as small as 600 sf and average units up to 1,750 net
habitable sf. Your lot size and zoning would likely clear those tests.

The obstacle is elsewhere. SB 684 bars any project requiring the **demolition
or alteration** of housing that is rent- or price-controlled, deed-restricted
affordable, or **occupied by a tenant within the five years before
application** — including housing since demolished or vacated. Rent control
alone triggers it. A 1947 four-unit building in the City of Los Angeles is
presumptively rent-stabilized, and if that holds, a scheme that clears the
existing building is barred no matter what you are prepared to pay.

**The caveat, and it is a genuine one.** AB 130, a June 2025 budget trailer
bill, authorized subdivisions to designate a **remainder parcel** that retains
existing structures and uses, contains no new residential units, and is not
exclusively dedicated to serving the new development. Remainder parcels do not
count against the ten-parcel maximum and are excluded from density
calculations. On its face that suggests a configuration where the existing
fourplex stays untouched on a remainder parcel while new units go on the
balance of the lot — and if nothing is demolished or altered, the
protected-housing bar may not be triggered at all.

We are flagging this rather than relying on it. It needs the statute text and
a written position from City Planning, and it is exactly the kind of reading
that is easy to get wrong. But it is the difference between "SB 684 is closed
to you" and "SB 684 may be open in a specific configuration," and you should
not be told the former without hearing the latter.

## WHAT NEEDS VERIFICATION

In the order that resolves the most uncertainty per unit of effort.

1. **Rent stabilization status, and the certificate-of-occupancy date.**
   *(LAHD + LADBS.)* This governs SB 684, CHIP, and any redevelopment pro
   forma. If the units are RSO, several pathways narrow sharply and the
   replacement obligation becomes the central number. Nothing else should be
   designed before this is known.
2. **One ZIMAS session on the parcel** — it answers most of the rest at once:
   zoning and height district, General Plan and community plan, overlays,
   specific plan, HPOZ, hillside, fire, environmental layers, prior case
   numbers, **and** the program-eligibility flags: `SHRA / SB 684 Eligibility`,
   SB 79 and Low-Rise eligibility, CHIP incentive area, TOC tier.
3. **APN and legal lot area from the Assessor** — searched by address, not
   taken from a search result. Every density and yield figure depends on lot area.
4. **LADBS permit history and the legal unit count.** The ADU cap runs off
   *legal* units, not occupied rooms, and this also surfaces any unpermitted
   work — which matters before you open a file with the City.
5. **Written LAHD position on whether new ADUs on a pre-October-1978 parcel
   become rent-stabilized themselves.** LAHD lists ADUs and JADUs among covered
   types. This is genuinely unsettled in practice and it changes the economics
   of the ADU pathway. Get it in writing.
6. **Recorded CC&Rs.** The Beverlywood Homes Association restricts member lots
   to single-family use. We could not establish whether Vidor Drive is a member
   tract, and an HOA website is not a recorded document. State ADU law voids
   some private restrictions but not all.
7. **Westside Community Plans Update.** Your community plan area is being
   updated and parcels will be remapped into the City's New Zoning Code
   (adopted January 2025). This is the direct answer to your question about
   changing requirements — your zoning designation itself is scheduled to
   change. Worth being on the notice list.

## MOST IMPORTANT NEXT STEPS

**First — settle the rent-stabilization question.** It is the hinge. One LAHD
lookup plus the LADBS certificate-of-occupancy date, and most of this brief
either firms up or falls away. Do this before design work of any kind.

**Second — run the ZIMAS parcel report and capture the program flags.** The
City has already computed SB 684 eligibility, Low-Rise and SB 79 eligibility,
and CHIP incentive areas for your parcel. Those are flags to read, not
determinations to argue. The **Low-Rise station-area flag** and the
**`SHRA / SB 684 Eligibility`** flag are the two that most change the answer.

**Third — if you are going to spend design money, spend it on the ADU
pathway.** It is the one route identified here that does not require
demolishing or altering existing units, does not trigger replacement-housing
obligations, and is ministerial. Start with a measured existing-conditions
survey and a yard-capacity study, not a scheme.

Two things worth saying plainly. The programs you named were largely written
for **vacant land or single-family parcels**. Your property appears to be
neither — it reads as already-built small multifamily. That is a substantive
reason those programs felt hard to apply, and it is not a gap in your reading
of them.

And the binding constraint here is most likely **tenant protection law, not
zoning**. That is an unusual place to look for a development constraint, and
it is where a zoning-first analysis goes wrong.
